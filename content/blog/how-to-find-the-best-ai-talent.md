**The short answer:** to find the best AI talent, look at the work before the title. Exceptional people leave a public trail of papers, launch posts, code, talks and case studies, so start there, then check every claim against independent sources. And look one or two levels below the headlines, because that is where a lot of the best work in AI actually happens.

That is the whole method. The rest of this page is how I do it in practice, with real people from my lists as examples. All of them are from the lists I compiled on November 23, and every fact below is sourced on their profile.

One thing first, because it matters. Finding someone excellent is not the same as finding someone available. Being on one of my lists says a person does exceptional work. It doesn't say they are looking for a new opportunity.

## 1. Start with launch posts and changelogs

Titles are compressed and often vague. Launch posts are specific. When a company ships something, the announcement, the changelog or the team's own posts usually name who built it. That is the single most useful place I know to find people who do the work.

- [[Johnny Dallas]] is named as the engineering lead on Figma Specs in Figma's November 23 agent launch post.
- [[Nora Kassner]], a Research Scientist at Google DeepMind, co-wrote the November 19 launch of Tool Retrieval in the Gemini API.

**Try this:** pick a product you admire, open its last three launch announcements, and write down every name in them. Then follow those names back to their own sites.

## 2. Read the author list, not just the abstract

For research, the paper is the profile. arXiv, lab blogs and research sites list who did the work, and co-first authorship is a strong, specific signal. Read past the famous last author.

- [[Chawin Sitawarin]], on Anthropic's Safeguards Research team, is a co-first author of Anvil, published November 12.
- [[Jan Dubiński]], a researcher at Truthful AI, is a co-first author of Homeopathic Learning, published with Anthropic on November 10.

Both are on the [[list:research]] list. Neither got there for being loud online.

## 3. Follow the code, the standards and the talks

Some of the best work never gets a press release. It lives in repositories, standards drafts, personal sites, podcasts and conference talks. These are slower to read, and that is exactly why they are underused.

- Standards work is a quiet goldmine. On my [[list:rising-stars]] notes, Jake Moxey is a co-author of the HTTP 402 payment standard draft, and Amit Handa built and explained the Universal Commerce Protocol.
- Talks and shows give you someone's thinking in their own words. [[Katarina Batina]] talked through Shopify's bets on the shopping experience on Dive Club in May, and [[Bella Nazzari]] discussed the first Cosign Awards on a16z's MTS show on November 19.
- Case studies count too. [[Annie Zhou]] documents her design work on Project Genie on her own site.

## 4. Verify with independent sources

A claim on someone's own profile is a starting point, not a finding. I look for the same fact from a source the person doesn't control: a company post, a paper, a public record, a neutral reference. On my lists, when I've confirmed a claim that way, it gets a Verified badge, and you can tap it to see how.

My favorite cross-check is to look at one launch from several chairs. If the design, product and marketing people all line up around the same release, you are looking at a real team, not a résumé.

- Replit Missions launched on November 17. [[Carmah Hawwari]] designed the experience, [[Samuel Spitz]] is on the product team behind it, and [[Tulika Gupta]] shaped its go-to-market.
- Genie 4 reached Project Genie on November 18. [[Annie Zhou]] leads design, [[Hang Qi]] co-authored the Genie 4 announcement, and [[Diego Rivas]] is the product lead who launched it.

**Also write down the date you checked.** Roles change fast in AI. A fact with a check date is useful. A fact without one is a guess.

## 5. Tell the rising people from the famous ones

Talent and fame are different axes, and mixing them up is the most common mistake I see. So every card on my lists carries a Visibility level, from Under the radar through Emerging, Recognized and Prominent to Iconic. It describes how widely someone is already known. It is not a talent score, and it never changes the order of a list.

- [[Jason Wei]], now Chief Scientist at Mercor, is Prominent.
- [[Katarina Batina]] is Recognized.
- [[Annie Zhou]] leads design for Project Genie, which added Genie 4 on November 18, and she is Under the radar.

If you only search for people you have already heard of, you will only ever find the top of that scale. Most of my [[list:design]] list sits at the bottom of it.

## 7. Know the signs that mean "keep reading"

None of these prove anything on their own. They just mean you haven't found the work yet.

- **One source.** If everything traces back to a single bio or a single post, keep digging before you decide anything.
- **A title with no artifacts.** "Head of AI" with no launch, paper, repo or talk attached is a title, not a track record.
- **No dates.** Undated claims are often stale ones.
- **Follower counts standing in for work.** Reach is real, but it measures attention. Plenty of the best people I track have small audiences.
- **Credit that doesn't match the launch.** If someone's account of a project doesn't line up with the company's own post, go read the company's post.

## 8. Keep a living watchlist

The best AI talent moves fast, so a list you made in June is already out of date. Keep yours short, date every entry, and revisit it on a rhythm that suits you. When someone new earns a spot, let someone else rotate off. That is why my lists stop at five, and why last week's boards live in the [[list:archive]].

Keep a second, looser list for people you are still watching. Mine is [[list:rising-stars]]: working notes on builders under consideration who aren't on the [[list:5x5]] yet.

## FAQ

**How do I find the best AI talent?**
Start with their public work, not their titles: launch posts, papers, code, talks and case studies. Verify each claim with a source the person doesn't control, note when you checked it, and look one or two levels below the headlines.

**Where do the best AI researchers publish their work?**
On arXiv, on lab and company research blogs, and on their own sites. Read the full author list. Co-first authors are often the people who did most of the work.

**How can I tell if someone's AI experience is real?**
Look for the same fact from an independent source, like a company launch post, a paper or a public record. A title with no launch, paper, repo or talk behind it isn't evidence yet.

**How do I find underrated or under-the-radar AI talent?**
Read launch credits and author lists instead of follower counts, and track visibility separately from talent. Many of the people doing the most interesting work are known mainly to their peers.
